# Accessibility Foundations

> DevsLibrary · Web Accessibility · Lesson 01

## Why accessibility matters
Web accessibility means people can perceive, understand, navigate, interact with, and contribute to the web regardless of disability, device, environment, or temporary limitation. It is not a finishing polish pass. It is a quality attribute that affects architecture, content, design, and testing from the beginning.

People may have permanent, temporary, or situational impairments: a person who is blind, someone recovering from eye surgery, or someone reading a screen in bright sunlight may all benefit from the same robust structure and contrast. A user with a broken arm, a noisy environment, a slow connection, or cognitive fatigue may encounter barriers that an implementation team did not anticipate.

## Accessibility is not one user type
Disability experiences vary. Relevant access needs include visual, auditory, motor, speech, cognitive, learning, neurological, and multiple disabilities. A person may use a screen reader, magnification, speech input, switch access, captions, keyboard navigation, reduced motion, custom colors, or no assistive technology at all.

Avoid assuming that every person with a given disability uses the same tool or wants the same accommodation. Build flexible interfaces and test with more than one method.

## Business and engineering value
Accessible interfaces can improve usability, search structure, resilience, mobile use, maintainability, and reach. Accessibility work also reduces the risk of blocking users from essential tasks. The ethical center remains the most important: people should not be excluded from information and services because of avoidable implementation decisions.

## A practical starting point
For every feature, ask:
1. Can a user discover it?
2. Can they understand what it does?
3. Can they operate it using their available input method?
4. Can they perceive the result?
5. Can they recover from errors?
6. Does it continue to work at different zoom levels, text sizes, and display settings?

## Exercise
Choose a common task such as searching a catalog or submitting a contact form. List barriers a keyboard user, screen-reader user, low-vision user, deaf user, and person with attention or memory difficulties could encounter. For each barrier, propose a design or implementation change.
